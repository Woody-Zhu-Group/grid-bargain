03 / UNDER THE HOOD

The decisions behind the numbers

The model follows a utility offering electricity capacity to a data center under rules set by a regulator. The central question is how those rules change the contract, the investment, and the amount other ratepayers are asked to cover.

How the game unfolds

  1. 01

    Set the rules

    The regulator sets the recovery share γ and allowed return ρ. An engineering assessment sets the eligible-capacity limit c̄.

  2. 02

    Make an offer

    The utility chooses capacity c and minimum-bill share α to earn the highest profit after operating and capital costs, while meeting the regulatory rules.

  3. 03

    Accept or walk away

    The data center compares its expected value of service, after paying its bill, with its outside option. It accepts only if the offer is at least as good.

  4. 04

    Build and serve

    If a deal goes ahead, capacity is built before demand is known. The data center pays under its contract, and other ratepayers fund the permitted recovery.

What shapes the outcome

Eligible capacity

The engineering limit c̄ caps what the utility can offer. It is not a promise that the data center will use all that capacity. Actual service cannot exceed the capacity built.

The minimum bill

The data center pays for the electricity it uses or its contracted minimum, whichever is greater. A higher α raises that payment floor, which can support utility revenue but make the offer less attractive to the customer.

The outside option

This is the payoff the data center can obtain without this contract. A stronger outside option makes acceptance harder to secure. It is the benchmark for deciding whether the deal is worthwhile.

Allowed return versus capital cost

The allowed return ρ is a ceiling on what the utility can retain on its investment, not guaranteed profit. Capital cost r is what financing that investment actually costs. The gap between them can encourage investment when recovery rules permit it.

Who covers the revenue gap?

The data center’s contribution is its payment after operating costs. Recovery share γ determines how much of the remaining gap to the allowed return other ratepayers may cover. Their payment T is an aggregate transfer, not a share of the construction bill or a per-household estimate.

Demand is uncertain

Capacity is chosen before actual demand is known. Lower use can leave capacity idle, while the minimum bill can still require payment for it. Acceptance and ratepayer recovery are assessed using expected values, rather than separately for every demand outcome.

What the illustrative settings assume

Service value and operating costs rise in proportion to electricity used. The investment settings control the base cost of adding capacity and how much more expensive further expansion becomes. These are adjustable examples, not estimates from the draft. Capacity and monetary amounts use normalized units, and all money flows refer to the same model period.

How to interpret the solution

Solve the game searches for the utility’s most profitable permitted offer that the data center will accept. It compares capacity choices numerically, so the reported best capacity is an approximation. If no investment beats walking away, the utility builds nothing, ratepayers pay no recovery, and the data center keeps its outside option.

Try an offer checks the contract you choose. A contract may pass the customer and regulatory checks without being the utility’s best choice. Offers that fail those checks are not implemented.

At full recovery, several feasible minimum bills can give the utility the same profit for a given capacity. The portal displays the lowest tied share. When investment would only break even, it selects no deal.

The regulator’s settings are policy inputs; this model does not choose an ideal policy. Other ratepayers bear the recovery payment but make no strategic decision in this compact game. Recovery is calculated in advance as an expected amount, not recalculated for each realized level of demand.
